A QUESTION WORTH ASKING

What does a
civilization leave behind?

Not only buildings. Not only monuments. It leaves ideas — written, illustrated, translated, protected and passed from one generation to another.

For more than a thousand years, Armenian manuscripts have carried stories of faith, science, medicine, poetry, law, music and everyday life. Their pages connect people who lived centuries apart.

This experience explores two institutions that safeguard this legacy: the Mesrop Mashtots Matenadaran in Yerevan and the Vatche and Tamar Manookian Matenadaran at the Mother See of Holy Etchmiadzin.

02 / A LONG MEMORY

A timeline of
survival.

The story of Armenian manuscripts is not a straight line. It is a story of movement, care, loss, recovery and renewal.

5TH CENTURY

The beginning of a written era

According to the Matenadaran's historical account, the roots of its manuscript tradition reach back to the 5th century, when Mesrop Mashtots established the Armenian alphabet and a manuscript collection began to form around the Catholicosate.

THE ALPHABET OPENS A NEW CHAPTER
1441

The Mother See returns to Etchmiadzin

The re-establishment of the Armenian Catholicosate at Saint Etchmiadzin laid the foundation for the growth of its library over the following centuries.

A LIBRARY GROWS WITH ITS COMMUNITY
1828–1913

Collections are counted and described

Manuscript cataloguing at Etchmiadzin began in 1828. The Matenadaran's historical review records 3,158 manuscripts in 1892 and 4,060 in 1913.

CATALOGUING MAKES MEMORY TRACEABLE
1915–1922

Protection in a time of danger

During the First World War, the Etchmiadzin collection was moved to Moscow for protection and returned to Armenia in 1922. The same period brought catastrophic losses to Armenian cultural heritage during the Armenian Genocide.

SAFEGUARDING BECOMES AN URGENT DUTY
1939

The collection moves to Yerevan

The manuscript collection was transferred to Yerevan. The Matenadaran's restoration department was also established in 1939, the year of the relocation.

A NEW CHAPTER IN CONSERVATION
1959

A research institute is born

On 3 March 1959, the institution received the status of a research institute of ancient manuscripts. In 1962, it was named after Mesrop Mashtots.

FROM REPOSITORY TO RESEARCH CENTRE
2012

A new home in Etchmiadzin

The Vatche and Tamar Manookian Library and Manuscript Repository was officially opened on 18 October 2012, supported by the benefaction of Vatche and Tamar Manookian.

A MODERN SPACE FOR AN ANCIENT COLLECTION
TODAY

Preservation meets new technology

Research, restoration, digitisation, cataloguing and public education help manuscripts remain accessible to new generations.

03 / YEREVAN

MESROP MASHTOTS RESEARCH INSTITUTE OF ANCIENT MANUSCRIPTS

The Matenadaran

A museum, a research institute and a remarkable storehouse of written memory.

The Mesrop Mashtots Matenadaran is one of Armenia's most important centres for the study and preservation of manuscripts. Its historical foundation is closely connected with the manuscript collection of the Catholicos of All Armenians at Saint Etchmiadzin. Before the collection moved to Yerevan in 1939, it was already known as a matenadaran — a repository of manuscripts and books.

The institution's present building was designed by architect Mark Grigoryan and completed in 1959. The research institute was established on 3 March of that year. In 1962, it was named after Mesrop Mashtots, traditionally credited with creating the Armenian alphabet.

The collection preserves manuscripts in Armenian and many other languages, including Greek, Arabic, Persian, Syriac, Latin, Ethiopian, Georgian and Hebrew. The subjects represented range from history and geography to medicine, mathematics, philosophy, law, music and literature.

06 / HOLY ETCHMIADZIN

MOTHER SEE OF HOLY ETCHMIADZIN

The Manookian
Matenadaran

A modern library and manuscript repository rooted in a centuries-old spiritual centre.

The Vatche and Tamar Manookian Matenadaran serves as a library, manuscript repository, research centre and venue for conferences. It was officially opened on 18 October 2012, with support from benefactors Vatche and Tamar Manookian.

The building was designed by architect Artak Ghulyan, who combined modern and traditional Armenian architectural styles. Its tall windows look toward Saint Gayane Monastery and Mount Masis. The library is used by seminarians, members of the Mother See, researchers and scholars from around the world.

Its collections include donated personal libraries, ancient and medieval Middle Eastern literature, and editions of the Bible in different languages. One especially notable item is the first printed Armenian Bible, published in Amsterdam in 1666.

A LANDMARK IN ARMENIAN PRINTING

The Bible
of 1666.

The Manookian Matenadaran's library profile highlights a particularly significant book: the first printed Bible in Armenian, published in Amsterdam in 1666.

It belongs to a different chapter in book history. Medieval manuscripts were copied by hand; printed books were produced using printing technology. Both forms preserve language and ideas, but they tell us about different stages in the history of reading, learning and publishing.

12 / BEYOND THE OBVIOUS

Six discoveries
to remember.

01

Translations can outlive originals

Some Armenian medieval translations are important witnesses to works whose original-language versions have not survived.

02

A note can become evidence

A colophon may preserve a scribe's name, a date, a location or a glimpse of the historical conditions surrounding a book.

03

One collection, many languages

The Matenadaran's historical overview lists manuscripts in Armenian, Greek, Arabic, Persian, Syriac, Latin, Ethiopian, Georgian, Hebrew and more.

04

Restoration is not rewriting

Conservators aim to stabilise an object while respecting its historical material and preserving evidence of its past.

05

A building can tell a story

The Manookian Matenadaran's architecture combines modern and traditional Armenian elements, linking its contemporary function with its setting.

06

Books travel through history

The Etchmiadzin collection's journey to Moscow during World War I and return in 1922 shows that preservation sometimes means protecting books by moving them.
